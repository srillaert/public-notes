# A Time to Die
Monks on the Threshold of Eternal Life  
by Nicolas Diat  
translated by Mary Dudro  
published 2019 by Ignatius Press, San Francisco  
original French edition : Un temps pour mourir: Derniers jours de la vie des moines: récit
published 2018 by Libraire Arthème Fayard, Paris, France  
read 2026-11-01

Diat visited 8 monasteries in France of different monastic orders. For each of them he wrote, for some quite openly, about the monks' experiences of dying and their understanding of death itself.

A documentary exploring similar themes is *Outside the city* about the Trappist monks of Mount St Bernard Abbey. Erik Varden, who appears as the abbot, also co-wrote a book with Nicolas Diat.

## Quotations :
I have included only quotations describing the Carthusian monastic life.

### Chapter VIII. The Deaths of the Recluses *The Grande Chartreuse Monastery*

Diat visited the Grande Chartreuse in July 2017 (p.153)

p.152
> The order founded by Saint Bruno is on of the strictest that exists in the Church: the Carthusians adopted the rule of Saint Benedict, but added to it the obligation of silence and abstinence from all meat (they only eat bread, vegetables, and water). Saint Bruno wanted to revive the ancient hermit life. This order is renowned for having never been unfaithful to the spirit of its founder, so that it never needed to be reformed.
> — Book with biographies of saints found by Diat at the library of Fontgombault

p.154
> At the Grande Chartreuse, singing is never accompanied by instruments. There is no organ. The only music comes from the works of nature.

p.162
> The Cathusians, including the sick, do not eat meat. The fathers do not eat breakfast. For half the year, from September 14 to Easter, the great monastic fast, they dine on a simple piece of bread with fruit juice or a glass [of] water. The only meal is lunch. In Cathusian language, they call it a pittance. It consists of eggs or fish and vegetables from the kitchen garden. During Advent and the fast that precedes Easter, the Carthusians abstain from dairy products.

p.163
> In the cemetery, there are no names on the graves. On one side, thin, black wooden crosses indicate the graves of the fathers and lay brothers. On the other side, stone crosses are reserved for the last earthly dwelling of the priors. The Cathusians choose to disappear from the eyes of the world and then from their own brothers. Often, they are incapable of finding the precise grave of a monk in the cemetery. The hermits die without leaving a trace.
